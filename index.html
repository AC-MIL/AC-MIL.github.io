From tissue morphology to kidney function

Routine measures of kidney function offer a scalable way to supervise computational pathology models. Yet a kidney biopsy contains multiple anatomical compartments, and their relevance changes with disease. Treating the entire slide as one homogeneous bag can dilute these distinct signals.

AC-MIL models glomerular-enriched and non-glomerular tissue as separate MIL streams. A learned bidirectional gate integrates their representations using the context of both compartments. On 696 PAS-stained whole-slide images from 347 KPMP patients, the ABMIL-based model achieves the strongest performance across all three reported eGFR regression metrics. Disease-specific experiments, transfer to proteinuria and albuminuria, and an exploratory pathology analysis support the value of preserving anatomical context.

  • Disease changes what matters

    The strongest single-stream compartment switches from glomerular tissue in diabetic kidney disease to non-glomerular tissue in acute tubular and interstitial disease.

  • Function-guided features transfer

    Frozen eGFR-trained representations support related renal endpoints. AC-MIL leads AUROC and accuracy for both proteinuria and albuminuria.

  • Distinct pathology signals

    Exploratory analysis of high-attention patches reveals different morphologic patterns in the two streams, connecting the architecture to tissue-level evidence.

The method

Preserve the compartments. Learn their context.

AC-MIL keeps local anatomical identity through pooling, then learns which features from each compartment matter in the context of the other.

  1. Separate the anatomy

    Extract 512 × 512 patches at 20× magnification. Frozen segmentation models identify glomerular-enriched patches, producing glomerular and non-glomerular bags.

  2. Pool each stream

    A shared, frozen CONCHv1.5 encoder maps patches into features. Each bag is pooled independently; attention-based MIL gives the strongest results in the main study.

  3. Gate in both directions

    Both pooled embeddings condition two feature-wise gates. The gated representations are concatenated and passed to a regression head for eGFR prediction.

Glomerular evidence + non-glomerular evidence → joint context → two learned gates → eGFR

Disease-specific experiments

Different diseases. Different predictive compartments.

The disease-stratified experiments reveal why anatomy matters: the most informative single-stream compartment changes with the dominant site of pathology. AC-MIL achieves the strongest correlation in both disease groups.

Pathology analysis

Two streams, distinct morphologic signals

What tissue patterns accompany the model’s predictions? An exploratory analysis examines high-attention patches from each stream, comparing patients with high and low predicted eGFR.

Glomerular tissue reflects a damage pattern

Lower predicted eGFR coincides with more VLM-identified inflammation, fibrosis or matrix expansion, injury, and structural distortion. Inflammation rises from 4.2% to 14.0% across the high- to low-predicted-eGFR groups.

Non-glomerular tissue carries a different signal

Injury or degeneration is more frequent in the high-predicted-eGFR group (9.9% vs 2.3%). This contrasting pattern suggests that the streams encode different aspects of tissue morphology.

Study design

Anatomy-aware learning, patient-level evaluation

696 whole-slide images · 347 patients
PAS-stained kidney tissue from the Kidney Precision Medicine Project (KPMP), with matched eGFR and at least 30 glomerular-enriched patches per included case.
Three patient-level folds · three seeds
Each slide is a separate input. All slides from the same patient stay within the same fold to prevent patient overlap between training and testing.
One shared frozen encoder
CONCHv1.5 embeds 512 × 512 patches at 20× magnification. Segmentation defines the glomerular-enriched and non-glomerular bags.
A shared training protocol
Thirty epochs with AdamW, learning rate 10⁻³, weight decay 10⁻⁴, and a Huber–Pearson objective. The main comparison includes mean pooling, ABMIL, and TransMIL.
